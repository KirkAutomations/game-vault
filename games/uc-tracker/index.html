Understanding Ulcerative Colitis Triggers

This app is built on peer-reviewed research to help you identify your personal triggers. UC is highly individual — what triggers one person may not affect another.

Key Research Findings

Stress (3.6x Flare Risk)

A UCLA study found patients with high perceived stress had 3.6 times higher odds of a flare. Stress releases cortisol and inflammatory cytokines, disrupts intestinal barrier permeability, and alters gut microbiota. The gut-brain axis makes this bidirectional — inflammation also worsens mood.

Diet (2x Risk with Pro-Inflammatory Diet)

A 2025 meta-analysis found pro-inflammatory diets doubled UC risk (OR=2.09). Red/processed meat, refined sugars, and food additives/emulsifiers are linked to flares. Omega-3 fatty acids and Mediterranean-style diets appear protective. During flares, low-fiber foods are generally better tolerated.

Sleep & Circadian Rhythm

Sleep deprivation increases IL-6 and TNF-alpha (inflammatory markers) and increases intestinal permeability. Evening chronotypes tend to have more severe disease. "Social jet lag" (irregular sleep-wake cycles) is associated with microbiota dysbiosis and more aggressive disease.

Air Pollution (~20% Increased Risk)

UK Biobank data shows higher PM2.5 and NO2 exposure increases UC risk by ~20%. Air pollution causes epigenetic changes (DNA methylation) at inflammation-related genes. Track your local AQI on high-pollution days.

Medications

NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) damage intestinal mucosa and are a common flare trigger. Use acetaminophen instead. Antibiotics disrupt the microbiome and increase IBD risk. Missing UC medication doses, even during remission, is a major cause of flares.

Gut Microbiome

UC patients typically have reduced microbial diversity with less Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Akkermansia. Probiotics, fermented foods, and adequate fiber (during remission) support a healthier microbiome. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics.

Nutrient Deficiencies

94% of UC patients are folate-deficient. ~30% have anemia. Vitamin D, B12, iron, zinc, and magnesium are commonly low. Low vitamin D is associated with worse disease severity and poorer response to biologics.

Hormonal Factors

Over 50% of women with IBD report worse GI symptoms during menstruation, when estrogen (which has anti-inflammatory properties) drops to its lowest level.

Infections

C. difficile is 8x more prevalent in UC patients. Any GI infection can trigger or mimic a flare. The Crohn's & Colitis Foundation recommends CDI testing during every flare.

The Partial Mayo Score

This app calculates your Partial Mayo Score (0-9) daily, the same metric gastroenterologists use:

  • 0-1: Remission
  • 2-4: Mild activity
  • 5-7: Moderate activity
  • 8-9: Severe activity
